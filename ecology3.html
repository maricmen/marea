Module 3 in one breath: a farm is full of living things acting on each other, and the professor's one rule is to learn the mechanism behind every interaction, because the mechanism is what you can manage. Week 8 gives you the processes: who removes what and who adds what, how niches and chemicals decide who wins, how communities change after every disturbance, and how populations grow toward a carrying capacity we can push up for crops and down for pests.
Read it top to bottom. Every interaction either removes something or adds something; competition and allelopathy are the two big cases. Over time those interactions reshape whole communities (succession) and set how fast each population grows. The dashed line is the loop back: once you know the mechanism, you can design the farm around it.

Start with the interaction. Odum's classic table names what happens to each partner: mutualism helps both, competition hurts both, commensalism helps one and leaves the other alone, amensalism harms one and leaves the other alone, parasitism and predation help one at the other's cost. The professor cares less about the label than the mechanism. Is the competition over light, water or nutrients? What goods and services does a mutualism trade? Some interactions remove something from the environment (competition, parasitism, herbivory), others add something (epiphytes using a host as habitat, mutualisms, allelopathic chemicals).

Then the two big contests. Competition happens where niches overlap. A species could live across its whole fundamental niche, but competitors squeeze it into a smaller realized niche, and over generations competition can even reshape a trait, as with the finches' bills. Crops have narrow niches and weeds broad ones, so farmers assume competition everywhere: row spacing, inputs, herbicides. Allelopathy is the chemical contest. Plants leach, volatilize, exude or decompose into compounds that suppress or, at times, stimulate their neighbors, and winter rye, velvet bean and cacao hull mulch put that to work against weeds.

Then time. Succession is the predictable change in which species are present, and any disturbance resets it. Old farm fields go from annual weeds to perennials, pines and, after 100 to 150 years, oak-hickory forest. Early colonizers are fast, short lived, poor competitors (r strategists); later persisters are slow, long lived, good competitors (K strategists). Farming is a disturbance repeated every season, which holds the field at the earliest stage and makes it depend on inputs.

And numbers. A population changes through births, deaths, immigration and emigration. Unchecked, it grows exponentially in a J curve; in a limited world it levels off at the carrying capacity. Conventional farming pushes crops past their carrying capacity with inputs and by removing competitors, while a resistant cultivar can pull a pest's carrying capacity down. The rest of Module 3 meets the players (soil organisms, insects, weeds, diseases), then how we manage them, then three case studies.

Week 9

The players

Quiz 09 · Sun Oct 18
Week 9 in one breath: meet the organisms that share the farm with the crop. Under the ground, soil organisms and nematodes are mostly allies; above it, insects are pests, vectors, predators, parasitoids and pollinators; weeds compete with the crop; and diseases strike when host, pathogen and environment line up. The thread through all seven lectures: know each organism's ecology and life cycle, because management in Week 10 works through it.
Read it top to bottom. The left column lives in the soil and the right column is the arthropods; both are mostly allies with a few serious pests. Weeds compete with the crop, and diseases need host, pathogen and environment to line up. The dashed line is the loop back: each management tool in Week 10 works through an organism's life cycle.

Start under the ground. A liter of soil holds huge numbers of bacteria, actinomycetes, fungi, algae and protozoans, about 10,000 nematodes, and fewer and fewer animals as size goes up. Only bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes and termites break down tough plant matter directly; the rest shred it. Together they speed decomposition, open pores for air and water, free nutrients and keep pests in check. Soil food webs run in a cycle back to decomposition, are fed mostly by plant matter, and are regulated by generalist predators: good for stability, hard for biocontrol.

Then the nematodes. Most described nematodes are beneficial decomposers, predators and insect parasites. The pests are the crop specialists, living around, inside or partly inside roots, or in leaves. Stunting, yellowing, wilting and root galls are the signs, and the root-knot nematode, with its huge host range, is one of the most important in the United States.

Then the arthropods. Know the four differences between arachnids and insects and the two life cycles: simple metamorphosis, where nymphs look like and live like the adults, and complete metamorphosis, where a larva that is usually the pest stage turns into a very different adult through a non-feeding pupa. Insects are pests, disease vectors (the psyllid and citrus greening), predators, parasitoids, pollinators and recyclers. At the order level, the homoptera are the all-pest guild: sap feeders, virus vectors, honeydew makers whose ants disrupt biocontrol.

Weeds and diseases. A weed is any plant out of place; agricultural weeds win with seed banks that last decades, good timing, local adaptation and, often, C4 physiology against C3 crops. Disease needs a susceptible host, a virulent pathogen and the right environment, and moisture is the usual trigger. Fungi cause up to 70% of crop diseases, viruses need vectors, and planting one uniform cultivar, as Ireland did with the lumper potato, invites disaster.

Week 10

Managing the players

Quiz 10 · Sun Oct 25
Week 10 in one breath: every player from Week 9 gets a set of tools. Living tools (predators, parasitoids, diseases such as Bt) and cultural ones (diversity, rotation, sanitation, timing) sit beside pesticides, which are fast and effective but kill beneficials, select for resistance and move off the farm. Diseases, weeds and nematodes each get their own toolkit, and none of them has a quick, easy fix.
Read it top to bottom. The left column works with living organisms and farm practices; the right column is pesticides, first their chemistry, then what they do on and off the farm. Below, the same tools are applied to diseases, weeds and nematodes. The dashed line is the loop back: pests and pathogens evolve resistance, so no single tool lasts on its own.

Start with living tools. Biocontrol is control by a living organism or its natural product: predators such as ladybugs, parasitism by parasitoids and entomopathogenic nematodes, and diseases such as Bt, whose cry proteins destroy the gut of specific insect larvae and do nothing in mammals. Classical biocontrol imports a non-native enemy for a non-native pest, filling the empty niche; conservation and augmentation work with enemies already there. Pest management adds vegetation diversity, mixed genotypes, pheromones for monitoring, and cultural practices such as rotation, sanitation and planting dates.

Then pesticides. We rely on them because they are fast, effective and the emergency measure in an outbreak. They are classified by target, origin, mode of action and chemistry, and their chemistry (Koc, water solubility, half life) decides where they go. On the farm they kill beneficials, leave residues and select for resistance, the pesticide treadmill; off the farm they leach, run off, volatilize and drift, disrupt hormones and biomagnify up food chains.

Then each player's toolkit. Diseases are excluded where possible, otherwise slowed: sanitation, durable horizontal resistance, rotation, drip instead of overhead irrigation. Weeds are managed with herbicides, cultivation and hand weeding, plus narrow rows, transplants and solarization. Nematodes are poor dispersers but almost impossible to eradicate once established, so their control happens before planting: sanitation, resistant or tolerant cultivars, rotation, fallow. Every tool has costs, and there is no quick, easy fix.

Week 11

Three case studies

Quiz 11 · Sun Nov 1
Week 11 in one breath: three real problems where Module 3 comes together. Honeybees are vanishing under many stresses at once, a beetle and its fungus are killing black walnut after jumping to a host we planted, and a wheat rust keeps evolving past the resistance we breed into the crop.
Read it top to bottom. Each box is one case study. Colony collapse is many stresses adding up; thousand cankers is a pest jumping to a new host and then into natural forests; wheat stem rust is a pathogen evolving past bred resistance. The dashed line is the loop back: every case uses the interactions, players and tools from weeks 8 to 10.

Colony collapse. Honeybees pollinate over 100 crops and add $14 billion to US crop production. In colony collapse disorder the adults disappear, with no dead bees, while capped brood and food stores remain. Suspects: the varroa mite and the deformed wing virus it carries, foulbrood and other pathogens, systemic neonicotinoids in pollen and nectar, and colony management (nutrition, travel, narrow queen genetics). There is no smoking gun; a synergism of sublethal effects is the likeliest answer.

Thousand cankers. The walnut twig beetle, native on Arizona walnut, carries the fungus Geosmithia morbida, its obligate associate. Black walnut planted as a landscape tree in the West gave it a new, highly susceptible host, where the disease is always fatal, and infected wood has carried it east toward natural forests. It cannot be prevented or cured, so the tool is stopping the beetle: do not move firewood.

Wheat stem rust. Puccinia graminis is the most important disease of wheat, cutting yield by up to 70%. It needs two hosts; clonal spores spread it from wheat to wheat, and sexual reproduction on barberry mixes genes and drives the evolution of virulence. Barberry eradication and the Green Revolution's Sr31 held it back until Ug99, found in Uganda in 1999, broke that resistance. Its microscopic spores ride the wind, which makes it a global problem.
