Module 3 in one breath: a farm is full of living things acting on each other, and the professor's one rule is to learn the mechanism behind every interaction, because the mechanism is what you can manage. Week 8 gives you the processes: who removes what and who adds what, how niches and chemicals decide who wins, how communities change after every disturbance, and how populations grow toward a carrying capacity we can push up for crops and down for pests.
Read it top to bottom. Every interaction either removes something or adds something; competition and allelopathy are the two big cases. Over time those interactions reshape whole communities (succession) and set how fast each population grows. The dashed line is the loop back: once you know the mechanism, you can design the farm around it.

Start with the interaction. Odum's classic table names what happens to each partner: mutualism helps both, competition hurts both, commensalism helps one and leaves the other alone, amensalism harms one and leaves the other alone, parasitism and predation help one at the other's cost. The professor cares less about the label than the mechanism. Is the competition over light, water or nutrients? What goods and services does a mutualism trade? Some interactions remove something from the environment (competition, parasitism, herbivory), others add something (epiphytes using a host as habitat, mutualisms, allelopathic chemicals).

Then the two big contests. Competition happens where niches overlap. A species could live across its whole fundamental niche, but competitors squeeze it into a smaller realized niche, and over generations competition can even reshape a trait, as with the finches' bills. Crops have narrow niches and weeds broad ones, so farmers assume competition everywhere: row spacing, inputs, herbicides. Allelopathy is the chemical contest. Plants leach, volatilize, exude or decompose into compounds that suppress or, at times, stimulate their neighbors, and winter rye, velvet bean and cacao hull mulch put that to work against weeds.

Then time. Succession is the predictable change in which species are present, and any disturbance resets it. Old farm fields go from annual weeds to perennials, pines and, after 100 to 150 years, oak-hickory forest. Early colonizers are fast, short lived, poor competitors (r strategists); later persisters are slow, long lived, good competitors (K strategists). Farming is a disturbance repeated every season, which holds the field at the earliest stage and makes it depend on inputs.

And numbers. A population changes through births, deaths, immigration and emigration. Unchecked, it grows exponentially in a J curve; in a limited world it levels off at the carrying capacity. Conventional farming pushes crops past their carrying capacity with inputs and by removing competitors, while a resistant cultivar can pull a pest's carrying capacity down. The rest of Module 3 meets the players (soil organisms, insects, weeds, diseases), then how we manage them, then three case studies.
