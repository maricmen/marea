Two elements, two stories. Nitrogen is run by biology: microbes shuffle it between four pools, and the forms plants like best (nitrate) are the forms that leave fastest. Phosphorus is run by chemistry: it is an anion that sticks to minerals, so the fight is not about losing it but about prying it loose. Every card in this guide is a piece of one of those two stories.

The nitrogen story, as a picture

gain (new N in) internal shuffle (nothing lost) loss from the field N₂O (greenhouse gas)
Tap any arrow or chip. One arrow adds nitrogen, six just move it around, and four take it away for good.

Tap a process to see why it matters, who does it, what switches it on, and whether the nitrogen is really gone.

Where it comes from. The air is four-fifths nitrogen and none of it is usable: N₂ has a triple bond that only two things break, the nitrogenase enzyme in a legume nodule (at a cost of 16 ATP per N₂) and the Haber-Bosch factory (about 1% of the world's energy). Everything else on the picture is nitrogen that has already been fixed once and is being recycled.

Where it goes. Organic nitrogen in residues, manure and microbes is useless to a plant until microbes mineralize it to ammonium. Ammonium is a cation, so clay holds it, but in any soil with oxygen the nitrifiers turn it into nitrate within days. Nitrate is what the crop mostly drinks, and it is also an anion in soils that have almost no anion exchange, so it rides out with the water. That is the professor's "mixed blessing": the form plants prefer is the form the field loses.

What the farmer is fighting. Three exits. Ammonia gas off the surface when urea hydrolyzes and the pH jumps. Nitrate leaching in sandy, rainy Florida. Denitrification to N₂O and N₂ when the soil goes anoxic with carbon around. Every one of these losses climbs exponentially once you fertilize past the yield optimum, which is why the whole course keeps coming back to nailing the rate, the timing, and the form.

The phosphorus story, as a picture

gain (P in) internal shuffle (P still there, just locked) loss from the field
Notice what is missing: no gas box. Phosphorus never leaves as a gas, which is exactly why manure and compost end up P-rich, and why the soil bank of P only grows.

Tap a process. The P picture is about two-way arrows: everything that locks phosphorus up can, slowly and unwillingly, give it back.

Where it comes from. Rock. Apatite, the same mineral as your teeth, weathers slowly and that is the only natural input. There is no endless atmospheric reservoir, so old, heavily weathered soils are P-poor and the P we add comes from mines (Florida used to be a big one; now Morocco and Western Sahara hold the reserves).

Where it goes. Almost nowhere. Phosphate is an anion that reacts with whatever cation is around: with calcium and magnesium when the pH is high, with aluminum and iron oxides when it is low. Those reactions make new minerals (precipitation) or stick phosphate to mineral surfaces (adsorption), and both get harder to reverse with time. The soil solution, the only pool a root can drink, is tiny and refilled from those locked pools. Availability peaks somewhere around pH 6.5 and even there it is "never particularly high".

What the farmer is fighting. Not gas, not much leaching (except in sand). The real losses are erosion, since P rides on soil particles, and harvest. The real problem is fixation: a fertilizer granule dissolves into a hot spot of phosphate that immediately starts precipitating and adsorbing. So the moves are about placement (band it next to the root, because P diffuses about a millimetre a day), matching the product to the soil pH (DAP for acid, MAP for alkaline), and letting biology help: mycorrhizal fungi explore soil the root cannot reach, and microbes recycle organic P faster than minerals ever release it.

N versus P, the five differences he asks you to notice

Ions in the soil

N moves as a cation (NH₄⁺) and an anion (NO₃⁻). P is only an anion (H₂PO₄⁻, HPO₄²⁻), so it interacts with cations and mineral surfaces, and barely moves.

Gas forms

N has three that matter (NH₃, N₂O, N₂). P has none worth counting (phosphine is ignored). No gas exit means P accumulates wherever it lands.

Main driver

N is driven by biology: every arrow but two is a microbe. P is driven by geochemistry: precipitation, adsorption, pH, Ksp.

Natural input

N: biological fixation from an effectively infinite atmosphere. P: weathering of a finite amount of rock, so old soils are depleted and young volcanic soils can be rich.

How much the plant needs

N is the nutrient we add most, by far. P sufficiency is around 0.3% of tissue, ten times less. And in the plant, both are mobile, so both show deficiency in the old leaves first.

Why 8:1 for the microbes but 20:1 for the tipping point? Microbes are heterotrophs: they take carbon and, like wood, use some to build the house and burn the rest to heat it. Up to about 20:1 the residue still carries enough N to cover the carbon they keep. Past 25:1 there is too much carbon, their carbon use efficiency drops, they respire more CO₂ and hold on to every bit of nitrogen, pulling it out of the soil. That is immobilization: temporary, not a loss, but fatal to a corn crop that needs its N now.

How he grades, in his own example

The question: Describe how a nitrification inhibitor improves the efficiency of N cycling (6 points). Read the four answers from the bottom up and notice what each one adds.

3/6half
"Nitrification inhibitors inhibit nitrification."
Accurate, but it neither describes the process in any detail nor says how that improves N cycling. Naming the mechanism is half the points.
4.5/675%
"Nitrification inhibitors inhibit nitrification and reduce N leaching."
Both halves of the question are touched, neither in enough detail. Right idea, no mechanism connecting them.
5.5/692%
"...inhibit nitrification and the formation of nitrate, a N form that is excluded from CEC sites by cations with a stronger sorption strength, therefore minimizing N losses via nitrate leaching."
Mostly excellent, but nitrate is an anion, not a cation, so it cannot bind CEC at all. One inaccurate clause costs you. Extra information is a risk, not a bonus.
6/6full
"Nitrification inhibitors inhibit nitrification and the formation of nitrate, a N form that is poorly retained in most soils and prone to leaching, therefore minimizing losses."
Mechanism (what is inhibited), consequence (less nitrate), why it matters (nitrate is poorly held, leaches), effect on efficiency (fewer losses). One sentence. That is the whole standard.
The answer recipe, from his instructions and the Sep 15 review
  1. Name the mechanism, then say how it works. "Oxygen" is not an answer; "nitrification needs oxygen, so it is stimulated in aerobic soil and inhibited in anaerobic soil" is. Same for pH: say whether the process runs at low, neutral or high pH.
  2. State the effect on the crop or the environment. More available? More lost? Which loss pathway?
  3. If asked to compare, compare. Describe each product, its effect on efficiency, then a sentence saying which is better and why. The three-part question is built this way.
  4. Match the number asked. Three factors means three (four at most, "just to be sure"). Seven is time lost and more chances to say something wrong.
  5. Stop once every part is covered. A few sentences, maybe a hundred words. "I don't want novels." Long answers lose clarity and pick up errors, and both are penalized.
  6. Reread for accuracy. The classic trap is above: nitrate is an anion. Check charges, check which pH direction, check which organism.
