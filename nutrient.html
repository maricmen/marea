Six transformations do all the work. Three are internal shuffles (mineralize, immobilize, nitrify), one adds N (fixation), and three lose it (volatilize, leach, denitrify). Know the equation, the organisms, and the controlling factors for each.

Why 8:1 for microbes but ~20:1 for the tipping point? Microbes respire a big chunk of the carbon they eat as CO₂ (they don't keep it all as biomass), so a residue can carry more carbon than 8:1 and still have enough N to spare. Above roughly 25:1 there is too little N in the residue, so microbes pull mineral N out of the soil to build biomass — that is immobilization.

Lability matters too. C:N converts everything to elemental C and N, but simple sugars and tough aromatic (lignin) structures with the same C:N decompose at very different rates, which is why the threshold is a gray zone, not a hard line.

Timing: the asynchrony problem

Even when a manure releases enough N over a season, it releases it gradually while corn demands it in a narrow peak. N released before the crop can use it (or after uptake ends) is exposed to leaching. Matching release to demand — and catching the tail with a cover crop — is the whole game.

Essential elements & availability

17 essential elements

Non-mineral: C, H, O (from air & water).

Macronutrients (≥ 0.2% dry weight): N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S.

Micronutrients (≤ 0.01%): Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni.

Beneficial (not essential): Na, Co, V, Si.

Stages of availability

  • Deficient — yield limited, visible symptoms.
  • Critical range — threshold below which yield drops.
  • Sufficient / luxury — no more yield gain, but tissue concentration can still rise (luxury consumption).
  • Excessive / toxic — high enough to cut yield via toxicity or nutrient interactions.

Law of the Minimum

Growth is limited by the nutrient in shortest supply relative to need, not the total amount of nutrients. Fixing anything else won't help until the limiting one is corrected.

Example: a field with ample P, K, water and light but low N yields as if only that low N were available — adding more P or K does nothing until N is raised.

How nutrients reach the root

Root interception

smallest share

Roots grow into new soil and contact nutrients directly — but roots only occupy about 1–3% of the soil volume, so this is minor.

Diffusion

immobile nutrients

Nutrient moves down a concentration gradient from high (bulk soil) to low (depleted rhizosphere). Slow. Dominant for immobile ions like H₂PO₄⁻ and K⁺. Faster with warmth & moisture.

Mass flow

mobile nutrients

Nutrient rides along with water pulled to the root by transpiration. Dominant for mobile ions like NO₃⁻, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, S. Needs soil moisture + transpiration.

Texture effect: fine (clayey) soils hold more water and nutrients, so mass flow and diffusion supply more; coarse sandy soils supply less and lose mobile nutrients faster. Mycorrhizae (AMF) extend the absorbing surface (hyphae reach ~8 cm out), which especially helps with immobile P.

Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)

CEC = the total amount of exchangeable cations a soil can hold on its negatively charged surfaces, expressed in meq / 100 g soil. It rises with clay, organic matter, and pH. Higher CEC = more nutrient retention. CEC = Ca + Mg + K + Na + H + Al

Two sources of negative charge

  • Permanent (isomorphic substitution): a lower-charge cation swaps into the clay lattice (Al³⁺→Mg²⁺ in the octahedral sheet, Si⁴⁺→Al³⁺ in the tetrahedral). Leaves a net negative charge that is pH-independent.
  • pH-dependent: deprotonation of OH groups on clay edges and organic matter. As pH rises, H⁺ leaves, exposing more negative charge — so CEC increases.

Lyotropic series

Order of how tightly cations are held on the exchange:

Al³⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺ > K⁺ = NH₄⁺ > Na⁺

Basis: higher valence + smaller hydrated radius = stronger attraction. Small ions hydrate more (bigger water shell), so Na⁺ is held weakly and leaches first.

AEC & why ultisols > mollisols

AEC (anion exchange) comes from pH-dependent positive charge on Fe/Al oxides & kaolinite at low pH. Ultisols are highly weathered (more oxides, more kaolinite, lower pH) so they carry more AEC than the less-weathered, base-rich mollisols. Anion order: PO₄³⁻ > SO₄²⁻ > NO₃⁻ > Cl⁻ — which is why NO₃⁻ is barely held and leaches.

Soil formation & profile

Soil forming factors — CLORPT

Climate · Organisms (biota) · Relief (topography) · Parent material · Time — plus Humans. Processes: additions, losses, transformations, translocations.

Horizons (O-A-E-B-C-R)

  • O — organic matter accumulation
  • A — mineral topsoil enriched in OM
  • E — zone of eluviation (leached out)
  • B — zone of illuviation (accumulation)
  • C — parent material
  • R — unweathered rock
Nitrogen is usually the #1 limiting nutrient because it builds chlorophyll, RUBISCO, proteins and nucleic acids — all needed in bulk. Deficiency and excess each have a tell.

What N builds

  • Chlorophyll — captures light energy
  • Proteins / enzymes, esp. RUBISCO — fixes CO₂ in photosynthesis
  • Nucleic acids — DNA & ribosomal RNA

Needed in large quantities → the nutrient we add most.

Deficiency symptoms

old leaves first

General, uniform yellowing (not edge or spotty patterns) plus slow growth.

Key diagnostic: N is mobile in the plant, so it is pulled from old lower leaves into new growth — so old leaves yellow first. An immobile nutrient would show on young leaves first.

Excess symptoms

too much of a good thing
  • Biomass goes to leaves over fruit (lush tomato, few tomatoes)
  • Delayed maturity
  • Lodging — weak stems flatten in wind
  • Greater pest susceptibility

Wasteful: more fertilizer, no more yield.

Uptake form

Plants take up both NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻, but nitrate usually dominates (most NH₄⁺ gets nitrified). Uptake is mostly by mass flow. Organic N (amino acids) is minor except in harsh cold systems (Arctic sedges). Exceptions prefer NH₄⁺: blueberries (acid soils), flooded rice.
