Module 2 in one breath: a farm lives on water it does not control and soil it did not make. Water arrives as rain or is pulled from rivers and aquifers, leaves as runoff, percolation and evapotranspiration, and every shortcut we take to keep it coming (overdrafting, diverting, irrigating dry land) comes back as subsidence, salt, dead zones or desert. The soil is the medium that decides how much of that water and how many of those nutrients the plant ever gets.
Read it top to bottom. The dashed line on the right is the loop back: the soil's texture, pores and chemistry set how much of the incoming water infiltrates, how much is held, and how much leaches away with the nutrients.

Start with the plant. Water is the other half of climate, after temperature. The plant needs it for photosynthesis, respiration and nutrient transport, and it tolerates a wide band of soil water (the broad, flat-topped curve) but suffers at both ends: waterlogging starves the roots of oxygen, drought closes the stomata and shuts down carbon intake. Water is renewable, the same amount as 100 million years ago, but only 3% sits where we can use it, and agriculture takes almost half of what the US uses and 70% worldwide, mostly without giving it back.

Then follow where it comes from. Rain forms when air holds more vapor than its temperature allows. Florida gets its summer rain from convective storms, the Cascades and the Central Valley from orographic lift, the Gulf coast up to a fifth of some years' rain from cyclones. For a farmer the annual total matters less than the distribution, intensity and predictability. Where rain is short you irrigate (furrow, center pivot, drip, in rising order of efficiency and effort), or you farm dry on under 20 inches a year with fallow rotations and stubble.

Where it bites. Overdrafting aquifers collapses their pores (subsidence) and pulls seawater into the freshwater lens; diverting rivers leaves the Colorado dead before the sea. Irrigating arid land without enough water to leach concentrates salts until sodium displaces calcium, the aggregates fall apart and the soil stops draining. Soluble fertilizer runs off (phosphorus) or leaches (nitrate) into water bodies and starts the eutrophication cycle that produces the Gulf dead zone. Push semi-arid land too hard with grazing, cropping and tree-cutting and it becomes desert; the Aral Sea is the extreme case.

So the module ends in the soil. The profile (O, A, B, C, bedrock) and the twelve orders tell you what a soil can do. Decomposition turns organic matter into the inorganic forms plants can take up, at a pace set by the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. Texture and structure set porosity, permeability and water holding capacity, and with them wilting point, field capacity and saturation. pH and cation exchange capacity decide whether the nutrients in that water are actually available. Every lecture below is one piece of this single story.
