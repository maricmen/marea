The ACF project in one breath: you get one stakeholder role, you learn the basin and the spreadsheet well enough to know a good deal from a bad one, you write a private strategy with a best deal and a least acceptable deal (Phase I), you negotiate flow contracts with nine other roles on a Canvas board for about two and a half weeks (Phase II), and you finish with a short survey and peer grades (Phase III). Kiker: "Everything in Phase I is to get you ready to do good in Phase II" (12.1).

How the grade adds up

Term project = 40% of the course (syllabus). Inside it: Phase I strategy, metrics and spreadsheet work 35%, Phase II negotiation 35% (peers 30%, quality of your flow negotiations 35%, Kiker 35%), Phase III survey 30%. The rest of the course: case studies 30%, quizzes 25%, graded discussions and surveys 5%.

Kiker's own framing: Phase I is "about 10% or so... a full letter grade" of the course, Phase II "about 1.5 letter grades", Phase III about a full letter grade and "a gift as long as you do it" (announcements Nov 2025, 12.1). Students who take the project seriously "make a B or much higher; those who blow this off struggle to pass" (announcement).

Ten roles, and you play one. Kiker's most basic advice: read your own role, then read everyone else's, "because you do need to understand where they're coming from" (10.4). In any group of ten, "at least three or four people are sympathetic to your area. Find them and cut deals with them" (10.4).
Ten roles, not eleven. The spoken transcript of 10.4 reads like two roles at the end ("a regional association of smaller cities and power companies in the middle... a local Chattahoochee River Association"). Every written list (10.3 and 10.4 slides, Module 11 and 12 notes, Module 12 page) has one role there: Middle Chattahoochee River Association (smaller cities and power companies), MID. The role description PDF titles it "Middle Chattahoochee / Lower Flint Rivers", and 12.1 says "ten potential groups". So the "Chattahoochee River association" in 10.4 is MID, split by the auto-transcript.

The usual camps

Keep water upstream

ATL, GA, LAN. "City of Atlanta and Georgia and Lake Lanier are often good pals because they're all kind of working together" (12.1).

Send water downstream

FL, ENV, NAV. "If you're Florida, the environment might actually be your big ally" (12.1). Navigation wants reservoir releases too (role description).

Can go both ways

AG, AL, MID. "The agricultural guys, state of Alabama, middle Chattahoochee. They can go both ways" (12.1).

Broker

USACE. Federal interest, "should not be advocating for any one state" (role description).

Conflict & perspectives

The ACF is a big river that delivers its water at the wrong time. Most of it arrives in late winter and spring; farms, lawns, lakes and the bay all want it in summer. One lake, Lanier, holds about two thirds of all the storage, sits at the top of the basin, and refills slowly. That is why everyone fights over it (9.2, 9.3).

      Reading a reservoir schedule

      Think of each reservoir as a bank account (Kiker's "Negotiation Strategy Tip 1" announcement): when the account is full you spend freely; as it drops you cut back. For each month you set where each zone starts (a lake level in feet) and how much to release (cfs) while the lake sits in that zone.

      Lanier zones in the model: Normal (top fixed at 1071 ft), Transition, Drought, and Dead (1035 ft, red, untouchable). West Point & George: top 637 ft, dead zone 620 ft.

      Try it (10.3's quick quiz). Kiker's example January to May schedule: Normal zone 1071 to 1065 releases 4,000 cfs, Transition 1065 to 1050 releases 2,000 cfs, Drought 1050 to 1035 releases 750 cfs. It is May and Lanier sits at 1060 ft. What is the release?
      Sources: 10.1, 10.3 and its lecture notes; Oct 2025 announcement "You are welcome to change ALL green zones".

      Metric recipes

      Count bad days. In RawDataForNegotiations, next to a flow column, type =IF(B5<5000,1,0), copy it down all 3,650 rows, then =SUM(...) the column. That gives days in ten years under 5,000 cfs (11.1). Change the threshold for other roles: 6,000 for Florida, a navigation flow for NAV.

      Compare rivers. Insert a column dividing Chattahoochee flow by Flint flow, then take the =AVERAGE. It shows how much of the low-flow burden the Flint carries (11.2, the Flint ag argument).

      Check a lake. Use =MIN on a Lanier or West Point & George level column, or count days under a recreation level such as 1066 ft for Lanier (10.1).

      Columns in RawDataForNegotiations: B, D, F Florida flow (average, moderate, severe) with C, E, G marking days under 5,000; H, I, J Middle Chattahoochee flow; K, L, M Lake Lanier level; then one block per scenario (P to U, X to AC, AF to AK) with the Lanier release, Middle Chattahoochee, Flint and Florida flows and both lake levels. Row 3 holds minimums; row 3657 holds the day counts.

      Sources: 11.1, 11.2, 11.3; v5.1 file, RawDataForNegotiations.
